## Toposcope and panorama

### Toposcope

- Access: Main menu > Toposcope
- Keyboard shortcut: <kbd>g</kbd> <kbd>s</kbd>
- URL path: `/#tools=toposcope`

Draws a toposcope (an orientation table / compass rose): a dial standing on one drawn point, with a named ray to every other drawn point, showing the direction and distance of each. The ◎ button along the bottom of its panel hands the map over to a single click that says where the toposcope stands — every toolbar and every floating panel goes away while it waits, the way picking a home or photo location does. Its caret offers the other way round: stand the dial on your own GPS position, turning on Locate me if it is off and waiting for the first fix. That click adds a **drawing point** carrying the property `toposcope=center` (and, cosmetically, the map's own viewpoint symbol); pressing the button again moves it. That property is the whole definition of the centre — not the icon, not the position, both of which are the user's to change — so restyling the marker or drawing another point in front of it means nothing to the dial. It travels with the point in the `point=` URL params, so a copied link brings the whole toposcope with it. Removing the property, or the point, ends the toposcope; every other drawn point is a ray. If more than one point is marked, the first wins. From then on the centre is an ordinary drawn point: label it, restyle it or drag it in the drawing tool. The toposcope and the drawing tools can be open together, so the dial fills in as points are added, and clicking a ray selects its point on the map — moving the map to it where the dial itself is over it or it is off screen entirely, and leaving the map alone where it can already be seen. Pressing the middle of the dial does the same for the centre point: the inner circle and the lines written in it turn the selection colour, the way a picked ray does, and the centre's own marker is selected on the map. The dial is drawn on an opaque disc so it reads against whatever map is under it, and sits in a floating panel that can be moved, resized, put full screen and closed. The floating panels — this one, the panorama, the elevation profile — stack: pressing anywhere in one brings it in front of the others, including a press that starts a drag, a resize or one of its own controls. All of them are translucent over a blurred backdrop, so the map still reads behind them without its labels and hairlines making the panel's own text hard to follow; full screen turns both off, there being nothing behind to see.

What each ray says comes from two **line templates** held by the dial, not from the point itself — first line `{label}` and second `[{elevation} · ]{distance}` by default, so a peak converted from the Objects tool reads "Kráľova hoľa / 1946 m · 12,3 km" with nothing typed into it. A template can name `{label}` (the point's own label, its property references already resolved), `{elevation}`, `{elevation_ft}`, `{distance}`, `{distance_mi}`, `{azimuth}`, `{location}` (the position as a toposcope engraves it, `N 49° 14' 9"` over `E 15° 34' 14"`), and `{p:<name>}` for anything else the point carries. A name the dial knows but has no value for expands to nothing and takes any `[…]` group around it with it — the same optional-group syntax a drawing label uses — so a point with no elevation shows the distance alone, while a name it doesn't know at all stays as written — a template is applied to every ray at once, so a typo swallowed there would be swallowed everywhere; the first template is written above the ray and the second below it, each keeping whatever lines its values bring — a multi-line point label reads on the dial the way it reads on the map, all of it above the ray — and a template that comes out empty leaves nothing on its side. The text inside the inner circle is the centre point's own label, which starts out as `{location}`. The ⚙ **Toposcope** settings, opened from the panel's control row, hold those two templates, the four inscriptions curving along the outer circle (`{attribution}` for the map credit, `{credit}` for the portal's own — "Toposcope by Freemap Slovakia" or "… Europe" by domain, filled into the N–E quadrant by default), the inner and outer circle radii and the **scale** of the writing (all sliders), and whether the western half's labels are turned so nothing reads upside down. The dial is one drawing in its own units, so resizing the panel scales all of it together; the scale is what changes the size of the writing against the rest of it. All of it rides in the `#toposcope=` URL param rather than in this browser, so a copied link reproduces the dial exactly.

The dial's controls are not in the app's top toolbar but along the bottom of the panel itself, next to the drawing they act on, scrolling sideways when the panel is narrow: ◎ to place or move the centre, ⚙ for the settings, a download button that saves the dial as an SVG for printing or engraving, and a full-screen button.

### Panorama

- Access: Main menu > Panorama
- URL path: `/#tools=panorama`, with `panorama=<lat>,<lon>`, `panorama-az=<degrees>`, `panorama-tilt=<preset|altMin,altMax>` and `panorama-fov=<degrees>` (written only for a slice narrower than the full turn, in which case `panorama-az=` is the direction that slice faces rather than the bearing being looked at); the settings modal is `/#show=panorama-settings`

Renders the view from a point on the map — all the way around by default — out of the same terrain models the elevation profiles and the outdoor map's shading come from. A single **View and detail** dropdown holds the two settings that trade against each other, each a slider: **Horizontal view**, asking for less than the full turn (All around, 180°, 120°, 90°, 60° or 30°), and **Detail**. Rendering costs roughly what it covers, so a 90° slice arrives in about a quarter of the time a full turn takes at the same detail — the one setting here that buys time back rather than spending it, or, with Detail set to **Maximum**, spends it on detail instead: the render is then as fine as the limits allow this frame, so the narrower the view the finer the picture, for about the same wait. Every option of this dropdown says how sharp the picture would be in pixels per degree and how many seconds it would take — the only place the horizontal view's effect on detail is visible. A slice has ends: the picture stops at them rather than wrapping, so it cannot be turned past them, and it never moves on its own (the compass/play toggle is off for a slice, there being nothing to turn through). Which way it faces is asked for, the way the viewpoint is: swing the wedge on the map and press Update. Narrowing the view frames whatever is on screen at that moment, so the picture does not jump somewhere unrelated. Asking to look at a place outside the slice is the exception that renders on its own — naming a place is the explicit act a render needs, so the slice swings round to it and the mark is kept across the render. The panorama's controls are not in the app's top toolbar but along the bottom of the panorama window itself, next to the picture they act on — scrolling sideways when the window is narrow, and reachable in full screen, where the panel covers everything else. The 👁 button among them — the same eye its map marker wears — hands the map over to a single click that says where to look from, the cursor turning to a crosshair while every toolbar and every floating panel goes away, the way picking a home or photo location does; its caret offers the other way round, standing the viewer on your own GPS position, turning on Locate me if it is off and waiting for the first fix. (The toposcope's centre is placed by the same pair, in the same order.) Either way, naming the place starts the render: it is seconds of work on a server that renders one panorama at a time, so it is never fired by moving the map. The panel does not take map clicks of its own, so it can stay open beside the route planner or the drawing tools. A crosshair button at the other end of the row asks for the click the other way round: it hands the map over to a click saying what to look *at*, and turns the picture already in hand to face it — the reverse of pressing the picture, which answers with a place on the map. It leaves the same mark a press does — the red crosshair on the terrain in the picture, and the crosshair, line of sight and distance/azimuth readout on the map — found by walking down the picture's own distance buffer at that bearing. A place that cannot be seen from the viewpoint is not marked where it was clicked: the mark moves to the nearer of the two places along that line that *can* be seen — the ridge hiding it, or the first terrain visible beyond it — so the map never claims a view of ground behind a ridge. Nothing is rendered for it, so it needs a picture to work on. **Create toposcope from this view** is a button of the row like the others. What can be done with the place itself is not there but in the boxes that name it, one at each top corner of the picture: a ⋮ under the **Viewpoint** elevation readout treats where the eye stands as a place like any other — the map context menu's whole list, and "Open in…" (without the panorama and look-at entries, the picture on screen already being both) — and the same ⋮ under the readout of whatever was last picked out of the picture does it for that summit or ridge. The row measures itself against the panel's own width rather than the screen's, so narrowing the window collapses it however wide the display is: labels drop to tooltips, and the plain buttons fold one by one into that ⋮ menu as soon as the row runs out of room — never before it — the toposcope first, then the ⓘ panel, the compass/play toggle, the ⚙ settings, and the crosshair last; full screen never folds, being worth most where there is least room. A ⚙ button opens **Panorama settings**, holding what is set once rather than adjusted while looking: eye height above the ground (for a tower or a drone), an exact vertical band when none of the presets frames what is wanted, **Unfold distance**, and the **Look** — Natural, Shaded relief (no ridge lines at all), Drawn, Engraved, or custom — the ridge lines' strength and thickness, the ridge colour, and the **Ground**. The ground is either one colour, which the built-in haze washes towards the sky with distance, or a **gradient** that paints the ground by how far off it is and replaces the haze altogether — the colour picker's own Colour/Gradient tabs choose between them, and it has R/G/B and hex fields like the ridge colour's. A panorama is drawn with the layered gradient unless you change it (that is what the Natural look carries; the other three looks are flat-ground and clear it). The picker's swatches are ready-made ramps — layered, aerial, poster, autumn, haze, spectrum, alpenglow, blue hour, sepia, woodblock, synthwave, emerald, copper and teal, ink wash — followed by the last few you built yourself, which are kept when the settings are saved. A gradient's left end is at your feet and its right end at the distance under **Gradient reaches** (Automatic, which measures the terrain actually in view, or a fixed distance up to the maximum visible distance; the middle of the ramp sits at a third of it). **Fade into the sky** makes its last colour the sky itself, so distant ranges dissolve into the horizon instead of edging against it, and turning it off gives a hard skyline. **Hide terrain beyond** — offered only once a fixed distance is set — leaves out terrain past that distance rather than painting it flat in the last colour, and summits standing on it are then not named. Unfold distance (0 to 20°, "True view" for none) raises distant terrain in proportion to how far off it is, so far ranges separate from the ridges in front of them the way they do in a hand-drawn panorama instead of being squashed against the horizon; the frame grows upwards by the same amount so nothing climbs out of it. Because the raising decides what hides what, it also brings into view summits a ridge really blocks: those are drawn faded, name and leader line alike, and rank below the summits that can actually be seen where there is no room for both; a **Name revealed peaks** checkbox in the Peak names menu turns their names off, and the ⓘ panel gains a line saying the picture is a drawing rather than a photograph. Both of those follow the picture on screen rather than the setting, so staging a different unfolding doesn't change what is said about the panorama still being looked at. **Maximum visible distance** sets the farthest terrain the render considers, from 10 to 400 km; past 300 km (which is what every panorama holds by default) it is premium's, every extra kilometre being walked along every ray of the picture. **Detail** is the picture's sharpness in pixels per degree, with stops from 5 up to whatever the current frame allows and **Maximum** as the last of them; the dropdown also quotes the wait each choice comes to. How finely the terrain is sampled behind each pixel follows from it rather than being a setting of its own. Without premium an account is held to the rays one render may cost (1800, exactly what a full turn at the coarsest detail has always cost): at a full turn that is 5 px/° as before, but a narrow slice is cheap in rays and so reaches four times the detail on the same allowance. The slider stops where the allowance does and carries a gem, and the button's label turns amber while the account is being held to less than it asked for. The seconds it quotes include the browser's own work — decoding the picture and its distance buffer — so they run on past the progress bar reaching 100%. Every one of those is asked of the renderer, so saving them stages the change and offers Update, exactly like moving the viewpoint — that is what decides the split: the panel's own toolbar carries what rearranges the picture already in hand, and the modal carries what has to be asked for again. Dragging the viewpoint marker afterwards, or changing the detail, the rays or the vertical view, stages the change and offers an **Update** button rather than queueing another render; the render in flight is dropped whenever a new one starts, so nobody waits behind their own abandoned picture.

The picture is turned by dragging it, and it wraps: a 360° render's last column abuts its first, so it can be turned indefinitely in either direction. The wheel (or a pinch) magnifies up to the image's own pixels. A compass strip along the top says which way the view faces, ticked every ten degrees and named at the eight compass points, and a wedge on the map turns with it, showing the slice of horizon currently on screen. The wedge belongs to the picture rather than to the pin, so it stays over the ground it actually describes when the viewpoint marker is dragged away; a faded eye appears where the picture was taken from the moment the pin is picked up, carrying that place's elevation, so the wedge and the sight lines stand on something. Both markers become one again once **Update** has rendered the new place. On a touch device the view moves by itself: where the device has a magnetometer it **follows the compass** — hold the phone up at a ridge and the picture looks at that ridge — and where it has none (a desktop, or an iPhone where the permission was refused) it turns at a steady six degrees a second instead. On iOS that permission is asked for by the press that names the viewpoint, since the sensor is only granted from a gesture. Turning the picture by hand takes it over for good, exactly as the ■ button does; the button beside it hands it back, drawn as a compass where the device has a magnetometer and as ▶ where it hasn't, since those are two different things to hand back to. Since a finger has no hover, pressing the picture on a touch device also leaves the distance readout there, and it stays over the terrain it measured as the view turns. The panel floats over the map — movable, resizable — and has a full-screen button.

**Peak names** are laid out by the app from the visible summits the renderer returns, each scored by the metres it stands above the terrain around it, weighed against how far away it is — a summit standing clear of its neighbours reads as a peak, one on a long level ridge does not, however tall it is. That measure is taken from where you are standing, so a famous mountain hemmed in by taller neighbours would read as unremarkable; the score therefore also weighs the summit's true topographic prominence, which does not depend on the viewpoint, discounted by how far the terrain model had to reach to attach it. That is what lets a panorama name Rysy from 4 km and Gerlachovský štít from 100. A name sits centred just above its summit with a thin leader line down to it, climbing a line at a time where the spot is taken and going unnamed where there is no room — so the names that survive a crowd are the summits that stand out most. Zooming in reveals more of them without asking the server for anything. Pressing a name marks that summit on the map — the same crosshair and line of sight a press on the terrain leaves — and the picked name turns red so it is clear which one is marked. Its name, elevation, distance and azimuth are then said in two places: floating in a box in the picture's top-right corner, and in a tooltip above the map marker. That box is the picked place's alone — the viewpoint's own elevation sits in a second box opposite it, top-left, so the ⋮ menu under the readout is plainly about the place picked rather than about where the picture is taken from. A press on the bare terrain has no name to give but still reports its distance and azimuth in both places, plus roughly how high it stands — marked `~`, since that figure is read off the picture's own geometry (the row's angle over that distance, the unfolding taken back off and the earth's curvature added back) rather than asked of the elevation API; a named summit shows the terrain model's own elevation instead. The eye marker carries its own elevation as a tooltip, which disappears while the marker has been dragged somewhere not yet rendered. The map also shows the slice of horizon the picture holds as a wedge from that eye, and **the wedge can be grabbed and swung**: under a full turn the panorama turns as it moves and settles wherever it is let go, which is the map's way of turning the view without hiding anything. Under a narrower horizontal view the wedge means the other thing there is to mean — it is drawn at the width that will be rendered, and swinging it stages that direction, offering Update rather than turning anything for free. Zooming into such a picture draws a second wedge inside it for the part actually on screen, so the map says both what was rendered and where in it you are looking; unzoomed, where the two would be the same wedge, only one is drawn. The eye under it keeps its own gesture — dragged, it moves the viewpoint. The crosshair the picture's own marks leave can be dragged as well: the view turns to it as it moves, the red crosshair in the picture rides the terrain it is being dragged over, and the distance and bearing in its tooltip and in the panel's own box follow it. Dragged over ground the picture cannot see — behind a ridge, beyond the horizon — the crosshair goes (nothing there is in view) while the figures keep reading, being of the place on the map rather than of the picture; letting go there still puts the mark on the nearest place that can be seen. All three wear the open-hand cursor that says so. A **Peak names** menu in the panel's control row holds the sliders that decide what is named — plus **Favour real mountains**, which says how much a summit's own topographic prominence counts beside how much it stands out from this viewpoint (0 turns it off; at the default a famous peak hemmed in by taller neighbours still earns its name), **Show elevations**, which writes each summit's height under its name in a smaller, dimmer face (every label is then two lines tall, so fewer of them fit — which is why it starts off), **Show distances**, which writes how far off each summit is on that same second line — beside the height where both are on, so turning it on costs no extra height — the **Name revealed peaks** checkbox while distance is unfolded, and a **Reset to default** for the lot — and all of them act on the picture already drawn, so each takes effect at once. Three of them order the summits against each other rather than cutting: Favour real mountains above, and these two. **Rank peaks by** slides from Size through Size and nearness to Nearness — whether a peak earns its name by how much it stands above its surroundings (the great mountains, however far off), by how big it looks from here (the middle of the slider, which weighs both), or by being close whatever it looks like, which keeps naming more of the near field until it runs out; nine positions carrying those five words between them, as the number-of-names slider does — and **How far names carry** is a haze distance running from 10 km up to 400 km and then to Clear air at the far end, past three times which nothing is named at all, since a name there would be pointing at empty sky; Clear air is last because it means no falloff and no cut, which is names carrying further than any figure on the slider rather than less far. The other two cut: **Number of names** says how many of them fit, from none through to as many as the picture will physically hold, and **Minimum dominance** says which summits count for one at all — the metres a top stands above the ground around it, negative where it never rises clear of its own ridge, with stops from Any through ≥ 0 m (only what stands clear) up to ≥ 1000 m. Which summits are named is settled across the whole horizon rather than across the view, so turning the picture doesn't change the answer — names come and go only at the edges. The two cuts are not the same cut: thinning by number keeps whatever ranks highest in each stretch of horizon, and rank favours what is near and seeable — a summit's score falls away with the haze, so at the default a giant on the far horizon loses to a hill two ridges away — while the dominance threshold keeps the serious summits however far off. The menu's button is named rather than summarised, since no two of its sliders would speak for the others; its icon follows the number of names. **Create toposcope from this view**, a button of the same row, **turns the picture into a toposcope**: the viewpoint becomes the dial's centre and each named summit a drawing point — `poi:peak`, with `name` and `ele` properties and a `{p:name}` label — so the toposcope's default ray templates read the name over its elevation and distance with nothing typed in, and the whole dial travels in the `point=` URL params like any other drawing. The toposcope opens beside the panorama rather than instead of it. Which summits become rays is worked out from the render, not from what is on screen, so turning or magnifying the picture first changes nothing: the same peak-name filters and the same "Number of names" setting decide it, read at a fixed magnification, and no two rays end up within five degrees of each other — which caps the dial at 72. Both ends of that setting land on the five-degree floor: the busiest step, and "none", which turns names off in the picture but does not answer a button pressed for a dial of them. Where the map already carries drawn points, pressing the button asks whether to append the summits to them or replace them; appending leaves a summit already drawn alone, so pressing it twice doesn't double the rays. Either way the dial's centre moves to the panorama's viewpoint, which the question says, since it re-aims any rays that were already there.

Every rendered pixel carries its distance, so hovering the picture says how far away that ridge is and marks that place on the map with a faded crosshair that follows the pointer; **clicking anywhere in the picture leaves the mark behind** — the bearing comes from where it was clicked and the distance from the picture itself. A dashed line joins each mark to the place the picture was taken from, along the line of sight the reading was taken — to that place rather than to the pin, so dragging the viewpoint marker doesn't tilt lines measured from where it used to stand. A mark that is already on screen leaves the map where it is; one that would land off it (or hard against an edge) centres the map on it.

**Quality** runs in five steps, from a coarse one that draws in about a second to the finest at six times its resolution and nine times its sampling, which takes the better part of a minute on a renderer that serves one request at a time. The coarsest is free; **everything finer is premium's**. Whatever is asked for, the coarsest pass is drawn first and the finer one swapped in behind it, so nothing is spent staring at an empty panel. A bar along the bottom of the picture — over it, so the picture doesn't shrink to make room — reports how far the render has actually got, the renderer saying so live rather than the app guessing; while the render is queued behind others it says how many go first instead, and a Cancel button beside it drops the render outright.

The ⓘ button says what the picture cannot show: the terrain model is **bare earth**, so forests and buildings are invisible and a view a forest would block is drawn as if it were clear — by far the largest source of error. Detail also varies by country, and the eye is placed on the highest point within a few metres of the click so a summit view is not spoiled by the rock beside it.
